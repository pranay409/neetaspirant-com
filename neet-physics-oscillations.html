Oscillations for NEET: SHM Concepts That Show Up Every Year

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 October 16, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Oscillations and Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) appear in 2-3 questions in every NEET exam — worth 8-12 marks. Yet most students either avoid this chapter entirely or memorize formulas without understanding the physics underneath. The result? You get numerical problems wrong even when you know the formula, or worse, face a qualitative question asking "Which graph represents displacement vs time?" and freeze. This chapter isn't hard; it just needs the right foundation built from NCERT Class 11 and 12.

Why Oscillations Matter More Than You Think

Oscillations appear not just as standalone questions but as part of larger problems in waves, sound, AC circuits, and even electromagnetism. A strong grip on SHM gives you the conceptual muscle to solve questions across the syllabus. NEET examiners specifically test whether you understand the meaning behind each formula — not just plug-and-play calculations.

From NCERT Physics Class 11, Chapter 14 (Oscillations), you need to master the motion equations, energy conservation in SHM, and the phase concept. Chapter 15 (Waves) builds directly on oscillations, so weakness here cascades. Most students score 5/12 in this section when 10/12 is easily achievable with focused preparation.

The Foundation: Understanding SHM from First Principles

Simple Harmonic Motion is not any oscillation — it's a specific type where acceleration is always proportional to displacement and opposite in direction: a = -ω²x. This is the defining equation. The negative sign is crucial; it tells you the force always tries to restore the particle to the equilibrium position.

The standard displacement equation is x = A sin(ωt + φ), where:

  • A = Amplitude (maximum displacement from equilibrium)
  • ω = Angular frequency (rad/s) = 2πf
  • φ = Initial phase (depends on starting conditions)
  • t = Time

Velocity and acceleration follow from differentiation:

  • v = Aω cos(ωt + φ) — maximum velocity is Aω at equilibrium
  • a = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ) — maximum acceleration is Aω² at maximum displacement

Many students memorize these but don't realize what they mean physically. When x is maximum, v is zero (particle momentarily stops). When x is zero, v is maximum (particle passes through equilibrium fastest). This is tested relentlessly in NEET through graph-matching and phase-relationship questions.

Energy in SHM: The Eternal Exchange

In SHM, kinetic energy and potential energy constantly trade places, but total mechanical energy remains constant. For a mass-spring system:

  • Kinetic Energy: KE = ½mv² = ½mω²A² cos²(ωt + φ)
  • Potential Energy: PE = ½kx² = ½mω²A² sin²(ωt + φ)
  • Total Energy: E = ½mω²A² (constant, depends only on amplitude)

This is where most students get confused. At maximum displacement, all energy is potential (KE = 0). At equilibrium, all energy is kinetic (PE = 0). The average kinetic energy equals the average potential energy — each is half the total.

🔴 Common Mistake Students Make:

Thinking that if amplitude increases, only kinetic energy increases. False! Total energy increases as E ∝ A². A doubling of amplitude quadruples the total energy. This appears in questions like "A spring oscillator is set in motion with double the amplitude. How does the maximum velocity change?" Answer: It doubles (since v_max = ωA), but the total energy quadruples.

Period and Frequency: Formulas That Connect Everything

For a mass on a spring: T = 2π√(m/k) and ω = √(k/m). Notice the period depends only on mass and spring constant, NOT on amplitude. This is critical — whether you pull a spring 1 cm or 10 cm, the time to complete one oscillation is the same. Yet NEET students often get this wrong because it seems counterintuitive.

For a simple pendulum: T = 2π√(L/g). Period depends only on length and gravity, not on mass or amplitude (for small angles). This is why pendulum clocks work — the period is independent of how high you swing it, making them reliable timekeepers even as the swing gradually decreases due to friction.

NEET questions test this deep understanding through scenarios: "A pendulum clock loses time in an elevator accelerating downward." Why? Because effective g decreases, so √(L/g) increases, making the period longer — each "swing" takes more time, but the clock mechanism still counts the same number of swings as a full second, resulting in measured time that's slower than actual time.

Phase, Initial Conditions, and Boundary Problem-Solving

The initial phase φ determines where the oscillation "starts." If you're told "a particle starts from maximum displacement," then φ = π/2 (or 90°), and you write x = A sin(ωt + π/2) = A cos(ωt). If it starts from equilibrium moving in positive direction, φ = 0.

NEET exams heavily test phase relationships. Two common patterns:

  1. Displacement vs Velocity: They're 90° out of phase. When one is maximum, the other is zero. This is why v = dx/dt graphs look like shifted versions of x graphs.
  2. Two oscillators: "Two springs with different spring constants oscillate with the same frequency." This is only possible if they have different mass-to-spring-constant ratios arranged such that ω₁ = ω₂, even though k₁ ≠ k₂.
