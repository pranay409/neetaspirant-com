Work, Energy and Power for NEET: Formulas and Frequently Asked Questions

By Ananya Sharma · 📅 October 14, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Physics

Work, Energy, and Power (NCERT Chapter 6, Class 11) form the backbone of mechanics in NEET. Out of 720 marks in NEET, Physics carries 180 marks, and this single chapter accounts for approximately 5–7 questions every year. That's 20–28 marks riding on your clarity here. Most students memorize formulas but struggle during problem-solving because they don't understand the conceptual linking between work, kinetic energy, and potential energy. This guide breaks down exactly what you need to know, shows you the patterns NEET examiners love, and answers the questions your textbook leaves hanging.

What Exactly Is Work in Physics?

Work is NOT what you do when you're studying hard. In physics, work has a precise definition: W = F · d · cos(θ), where F is force, d is displacement, and θ is the angle between force and displacement.

The critical insight: work depends on the component of force in the direction of motion. If you push a box horizontally with 10 N and it moves 5 meters horizontally, work done is W = 10 × 5 × cos(0°) = 50 J. But if you push at a 90° angle to motion (perpendicular), cos(90°) = 0, so work = 0 J. This is why carrying a load on your head while walking does zero thermodynamic work—the force is vertical, motion is horizontal.

NEET examiners test this through real-world scenarios: a person walking up stairs (work against gravity), friction during motion (negative work), tension in a string (perpendicular to motion, zero work). When you see a problem, immediately identify the direction of force relative to displacement.

Key Cases You Must Know

  • Force parallel to displacement: W = F × d (maximum work)
  • Force perpendicular to displacement: W = 0 (e.g., normal force, tension in circular motion)
  • Force opposite to displacement: W = −F × d (negative work; friction removes energy)
  • Force at angle θ: W = F × d × cos(θ)
Common NEET Trap: Confusing Work with Effort

Students often say "I did work carrying this box upstairs," but in physics, if the box moves horizontally while you hold it vertical, you did zero work on the box—gravity did negative work, and your muscles did positive work (which is biochemistry, not classical mechanics). NEET questions deliberately use everyday language to trick you. Always calculate work using the physics formula, not intuition.

Kinetic Energy and the Work-Energy Theorem

Kinetic energy (KE) is the energy of motion: KE = ½mv². The game-changer is the Work-Energy Theorem: Net Work Done = Change in Kinetic Energy, or W_net = ΔKE = ½mv_f² − ½mv_i².

This theorem is the single most powerful problem-solving tool in mechanics. Instead of using kinematic equations, you can jump straight to energy. Example: A 2 kg block starts at rest and a 10 N force pushes it 4 meters. Final velocity? Using W = ΔKE: 10 × 4 = ½ × 2 × v² → 40 = v² → v = √40 m/s. No need for a = F/m or v² = u² + 2as.

NEET loves this because it works even when forces vary or the path is curved. A 5 kg ball thrown upward at 20 m/s reaches a height where its speed becomes 10 m/s. Work done by gravity? W_gravity = ½ × 5 × (10²) − ½ × 5 × (20²) = 250 − 1000 = −750 J. Negative because gravity opposes upward motion.

Why This Matters for NEET

At least 2–3 questions per exam directly use the work-energy theorem. Another 2–3 use it indirectly (conservation of energy problems). Your speed in applying this theorem directly affects your score.

Potential Energy and Conservative Forces

Potential energy (PE) is stored energy due to position or configuration. Gravitational PE = mgh (at Earth's surface). Elastic PE in a spring = ½kx². The crucial principle: for conservative forces (gravity, springs), the work done is independent of the path. A 1 kg mass lifted 10 meters vertically or along a ramp loses the same PE: mgh = 1 × 10 × 10 = 100 J.

This leads to Conservation of Mechanical Energy: E_total = KE + PE = constant (when only conservative forces act). A ball dropped from 20 meters has initial PE = mgh = m × 10 × 20 = 200m J and KE = 0. Just before hitting ground, PE = 0 and KE = 200m J. At any height h during fall, ½mv² + mgh = 200m, so v = √(2g(20−h)).

NEET examiners test this through scenarios with friction or air resistance (non-conservative forces), where mechanical energy is not conserved. A block sliding down a 37° incline with friction loses energy as heat. The work-energy theorem still holds: W_gravity + W_friction = ΔKE. This separation is critical.

Exam Pattern Insight

Roughly 40% of work-energy questions involve conservation of mechanical energy (frictionless pulleys, pendulums, loops). Another 40% involve non-conservative forces (friction, air resistance). The last 20% test your ability to connect work, energy, and dynamics together (e.g., a block pushed up a ramp against friction and gravity).

Power: The Rate of Doing Work

Power is how fast work is done: P = W/t, measured in Watts (1 W = 1 J/s). But NEET loves the instantaneous form: P = F · v = Fv cos(θ), where v is velocity. This is crucial because it connects force, velocity, and power in a single equation.

Example: A 1500 kg car accelerates at constant 2 m/s² on a horizontal road. Resistance force = 500 N. At v = 20 m/s, what is the driving force and power? Applied force = ma + resistance = 1500 × 2 + 500 = 3500 N. Power = Fv = 3500 × 20 = 70
