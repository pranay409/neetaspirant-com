Chemical Bonding for NEET: VSEPR Theory and Hybridization Explained

By Rohan Verma · 📅 October 23, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

If molecular geometry and bonding make you nervous during NEET practice tests, you're not alone. Chemical bonding—specifically VSEPR theory and hybridization—accounts for approximately 8-12 marks in NEET and appears across organic, inorganic, and physical chemistry questions. More importantly, understanding these concepts prevents errors across 30+ related questions on the actual exam. This guide breaks down exactly what NEET examiners test, how to visualize it without guessing, and the shortcuts toppers use to solve these questions in under 90 seconds.

What is VSEPR Theory and Why NEET Tests It

VSEPR stands for Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory, covered in NCERT Chemistry Class 11, Chapter 4 (Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure). The core principle is simple: electron pairs (both bonding and non-bonding) around a central atom repel each other and arrange themselves to be as far apart as possible. This repulsion determines molecular geometry—which directly affects polarity, reactivity, and physical properties.

NEET examiners test VSEPR through three recurring question types: (1) predict the molecular shape given a Lewis structure, (2) identify which molecule is planar or tetrahedral, and (3) determine bond angles in given structures. Class 11 NCERT covers approximately 12-15 example molecules (CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆), and NEET typically asks about 2-3 of these directly, plus 3-4 structurally similar molecules that use the same VSEPR logic.

The key insight: NEET doesn't ask you to derive VSEPR from first principles. It tests whether you can apply it consistently. You must memorize the arrangement patterns for 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 electron pairs, then apply them instantly.

The Five Electron Pair Geometries You Must Memorize

  • 2 electron pairs: Linear (180°) — Example: BeCl₂, CO₂
  • 3 electron pairs: Trigonal planar (120°) — Example: BF₃, NO₃⁻
  • 4 electron pairs: Tetrahedral (109.5°) — Example: CH₄, NH₄⁺, CCl₄
  • 5 electron pairs: Trigonal bipyramidal (axial 180°, equatorial 120°) — Example: PCl₅, AsF₅
  • 6 electron pairs: Octahedral (90° all angles) — Example: SF₆, [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻
⚠️ Most Common NEET Mistake:

Students confuse electron geometry (includes lone pairs) with molecular geometry (only bonding pairs). For NH₃, electron geometry is tetrahedral (4 electron pairs), but molecular geometry is trigonal pyramidal (3 bonded atoms). NEET asks for molecular geometry 70% of the time—focus on the atoms, not the electrons.

Hybridization: The Second Pillar of Bonding

Hybridization explains how atomic orbitals mix to form new hybrid orbitals, allowing atoms to form more bonds and at different angles than pure atomic orbitals would suggest. NCERT Chapter 4 covers sp, sp², and sp³ hybridization, with rare questions on sp³d and sp³d² in inorganic compounds.

NEET tests hybridization by asking: (1) identify the hybridization of a specific atom in a given molecule, (2) match hybridization to molecular geometry, or (3) use hybridization to predict bond angles. About 4-6 marks come from hybridization questions annually.

The direct link between hybridization and VSEPR is this: the type of hybridization an atom undergoes determines its electron pair geometry, which determines molecular geometry. Memorize this connection:

  • sp hybridization: 2 electron pairs → linear molecule, 180° bonds
  • sp² hybridization: 3 electron pairs → trigonal planar, 120° bonds
  • sp³ hybridization: 4 electron pairs → tetrahedral, 109.5° bonds
  • sp³d hybridization: 5 electron pairs → trigonal bipyramidal, mixed angles
  • sp³d² hybridization: 6 electron pairs → octahedral, 90° bonds

For example, in CO₂, carbon forms two double bonds with oxygen atoms. Carbon's electron pair geometry is linear (2 bonding regions, 0 lone pairs), so it undergoes sp hybridization. This explains why CO₂ is linear with 180° C=O bonds—not because we're guessing, but because the hybridization necessitates it.

Quick Hybridization Prediction Shortcut

Count the number of electron groups (bonding regions + lone pairs) around the central atom using a Lewis structure. That number directly tells you the hybridization: 2 groups = sp, 3 groups = sp², 4 groups = sp³, 5 groups = sp³d, 6 groups = sp³d². This method works for 95% of NEET questions and eliminates the need to visualize orbitals.

Applying VSEPR + Hybridization to Real NEET Questions

The actual NEET exam combines these concepts with polarity, electronegativity, and reactivity. Here's a worked example from recent NEET papers:

Example Question: "Which of the following molecules is non-polar?" (A) CH₃Cl (B) NH₃ (C) BF₃ (D) H₂O

Solution using VSEPR + Hybridization:

  • CH₃Cl: sp³ hybridization, tetrahedral, but Cl and H have different electronegativities → polar
  • NH₃: sp³ hybridization, trigonal pyramidal with 1 lone pair, asymmetric → polar
  • BF₃: sp² hybridization, trigonal planar, symmetric, three B-F bonds cancel → non-polar ✓
  • H₂O: sp³
