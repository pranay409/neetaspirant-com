S-Block Elements for NEET: Alkali and Alkaline Earth Metals

By Arjun Mehta · 📅 November 26, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

S-block elements consistently appear across 8–12 marks in NEET chemistry over the past three years. Most students treat Group 1 (alkali metals) and Group 2 (alkaline earth metals) as a memorisation grind, cramming formulas without understanding trends. This costs marks during application-based questions that test your reasoning about reactivity patterns, hydration energies, and salt formation. If you're scoring below 90% on s-block practice tests, the problem isn't your memory—it's that you're missing the chemical logic that connects every property in this family.

What Are S-Block Elements and Why They Matter in NEET

S-block elements (NCERT Class 11, Chapter 10: "The s-Block Elements") occupy Groups 1 and 2 of the periodic table, defined by filling of their outermost s-orbital. Group 1 contains alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr); Group 2 holds alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra). These 12 elements sit at the chemical frontier—they're the most reactive metals on Earth, and their reactions form the foundation of inorganic chemistry at the NEET level.

NEET examiners ask three core question types on s-block: (1) comparing properties within a group, (2) explaining why anomalies occur (like lithium's unusual behavior), and (3) predicting salt compositions and reactivity in real scenarios. Without mastering the why behind trends, you'll guess on 40% of questions and lose marks unnecessarily.

The weightage matters: typically 2–3 direct questions, plus another 2–3 embedded in multi-step reaction sequences. If you're aiming for a 650+ score, you can't afford to drop even one s-block mark through carelessness.

Trends in Alkali Metals: Reactivity, Ionisation Energy, and Atomic Radius

Group 1 reactivity increases steeply down the group: Li < Na < K < Rb < Cs. The reason isn't intuition—it's physics. As you descend, atomic radius grows, the valence electron sits farther from the nucleus, and ionisation energy drops sharply. Lithium's ionisation energy is 520 kJ/mol; sodium's is 495 kJ/mol; potassium's is 419 kJ/mol. By the time you reach cesium, it's 376 kJ/mol. Each step down the group, the energy cost to remove one electron decreases by roughly 50–100 kJ/mol.

This ionisation trend directly controls reactivity: lower ionisation energy means the atom loses its electron more readily, so it reacts faster and more vigorously. Cesium is so reactive it ignites spontaneously in air; lithium, by contrast, can be cut with a knife but doesn't burst into flame instantly like sodium does.

Hydration energy also follows a trend, but it's opposite to ionisation energy. Smaller cations (like Li⁺) have higher hydration energy because the charge density is greater—the ion polarises water molecules more intensely. This is why lithium salts often behave differently from other alkali salts: LiCl is deliquescent; NaCl isn't. LiOH is more covalent than NaOH. These anomalies appear in NEET reasoning questions, and they all trace back to Li⁺'s small size and high polarising power.

Anomalous Behavior of Lithium: The Diagonal Relationship

Lithium is the odd one out in Group 1, and examiners love to test this. Lithium resembles magnesium (diagonal to it on the periodic table) more than it resembles sodium. Why? Both Li and Mg have high charge-to-size ratios, high polarising power, and high electronegativity relative to their group members. This "diagonal relationship" explains why:

  • LiCl is deliquescent (hygroscopic), like MgCl₂, but NaCl isn't
  • Lithium nitride (Li₃N) exists; sodium nitride (Na₃N) doesn't under normal conditions
  • Lithium forms peroxides (Li₂O₂) more readily than other alkali metals form peroxides
  • LiOH is weakly basic and partially covalent; NaOH is strongly ionic and strongly basic

NEET questions often ask: "Which of the following properties is anomalous for lithium?" or "LiCl is hygroscopic, but NaCl isn't. Explain." The answer lies in hydration energy and polarisation. Memorising these facts won't help; understanding the charge-density reasoning behind each one will carry you through every variation of these questions.

⚠️ Common NEET Mistake: Assuming all Group 1 elements behave identically

Students often treat Li, Na, K as interchangeable. They're not. Lithium's high polarising power makes it unique. When a question asks why lithium's salts differ from sodium's, your answer must reference atomic size and charge density, not just "lithium is different." Practice explaining Li's anomalies in writing—this trains your reasoning for similar questions about other s-block elements.

Alkaline Earth Metals: Reactivity, Stability, and Real Exam Patterns

Group 2 (alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) shows a smoother trend than Group 1 because even the most reactive member (barium) isn't as wildly reactive as cesium. Reactivity increases down the group: Be < Mg < Ca < Sr < Ba. However, beryllium's chemistry is so different from the rest that it's often grouped with aluminum in terms of behavior—another diagonal relationship. Beryllium hydroxide is amphoteric; magnesium hydroxide is weakly basic. Beryllium chloride is covalent; magnesium chloride is ionic but partially hydrolysed.

For Group 2 oxides and hydroxides, thermal stability follows a predictable pattern. Thermal stability of hydroxides: Mg(OH)₂ < Ca(OH)₂ < Sr(OH)₂ < Ba(OH)₂. As you move down, cation size increases, and the M-O bond becomes longer and weaker... actually, wait. That's backwards from what students expect. The correct trend is that thermal stability increases down the group because the larger cation better accommodates the charge of the oxide ion. So Ba(OH)₂ is more thermally stable than Mg(OH)₂. NEET questions test whether you can reverse this intuition correctly.

Solubility trends in Group 2 show a classic pattern: Group 2 hydroxides increase in solubility down the group (Mg(OH)₂ is least soluble, Ba(OH)₂ is most soluble). Group 2 sulfates do the opposite—they decrease in solubility down the group (MgSO₄ is very soluble, BaSO₄ is insoluble). This BaSO₄ insolubility is famous and appears in almost every NEET paper: barium salts are toxic, but BaSO₄ is so insoluble that it's used as a barium meal in X-ray imaging. These solubility differences rest on lattice energy and hydration energy—lattice energy wins when the cation is small (Mg), but hydration energy wins when the cation is large (Ba).

Oxides, Peroxides, and Superoxides: Know the Differences

This section separates strong NEET scorers from weak ones. Alkali metals form three types of oxygen compounds, and each has a specific stoichiometry:

  • Oxides (M₂O): Formed when alkali metals burn in limited oxygen. Lithium preferentially forms Li₂O. The oxidation state of oxygen is -2.
  • Peroxides (M₂O₂): Formed when alkali metals burn in excess oxygen. Sodium forms Na₂O₂ readily. The oxidation state of oxygen is -1 (not -2). Peroxides react with water to give hydrogen peroxide: Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂O₂.
  • Superoxides (MO₂): Formed when potassium, rubidium, or cesium burn in excess oxygen. Oxygen's oxidation state is -1/2 (or formally, O₂⁻ with one unpaired electron). Superoxides react with water and CO₂: 2KO₂ + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂O₂ + O₂.

NEET loves asking: "Which compound forms when sodium burns in excess oxygen?" (Answer: Na₂O₂, not NaO₂.) "What is the oxidation state of oxygen in KO₂?" (Answer: -1/2 or treat it as a superoxide ion O₂⁻.) "BaO₂ reacts with dilute H₂SO₄. Write the equation and identify the product."
