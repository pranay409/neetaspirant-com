Halogens and Noble Gases for NEET: Reactivity Trends and Exam Patterns

By Rohan Verma · 📅 November 26, 2026 · ⏱ 8 min read · 🎯 NEET Chemistry

Halogens and noble gases form the two ends of Group 17 and Group 18 of the periodic table—and they're textbook opposites in terms of chemical behavior. If you've struggled to remember why fluorine is so reactive while argon barely reacts at all, or if you're unsure which halogen displacement reactions actually appear on NEET, this guide cuts through the confusion. NEET chemistry typically tests 3-4 marks on halogen reactivity trends and 2-3 marks on noble gas properties and applications. Getting this right can be the difference between 680 and 720 on your chemistry score.

Understanding Halogen Reactivity Trends

NCERT Class 12 Chapter 10 (The P-Block Elements) dedicates significant space to halogens, specifically focusing on their periodic properties and reactivity patterns. The core concept is simple but frequently tested: halogen reactivity decreases as you go down the group. Fluorine (F) is the most reactive nonmetal known; chlorine (Cl) is still highly reactive; bromine (Br) is less so; iodine (I) is the least reactive of the common halogens.

Why this trend matters for NEET: This isn't just memorization. The underlying reason—the balance between electronegativity and atomic radius—determines whether a halogen can displace another. A more reactive halogen will always displace a less reactive one from its salt solution. So if you see a question asking which halogen can oxidize iodide ions (I⁻) to iodine, the answer is F₂, Cl₂, or Br₂, but never I₂. This type of reasoning question appears at least once every two years in NEET.

Atomic and Ionic Properties

The most frequently tested property in NEET is ionic radius. When halogens form anions (F⁻, Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻), their size increases down the group. This seems counterintuitive since atomic size increases, but once you have an extra electron, it's added to a new electron shell. NEET tests this through multi-step questions: "Which halide ion has the smallest ionic radius?" Answer: F⁻. "Which halogen forms the most stable ionic compound with sodium?" Answer: F, because Na⁺F⁻ benefits most from the strong electrostatic attraction between small Na⁺ and very small F⁻.

Bond dissociation energy is another key concept. Fluorine is anomalous here—its F-F bond is actually weaker than the Cl-Cl bond, despite fluorine being more reactive overall. This is because F atoms are very small, causing electron-electron repulsion in the F-F bond. NEET uses this to ask tricky questions: "Why is F₂ more reactive than Cl₂ if F-F bond energy is lower?" The answer involves overall thermodynamics and the extreme electronegativity of fluorine, not just bond strength alone.

Halogen Chemistry: Displacement and Redox Reactions

Approximately 60-70% of NEET questions on halogens involve displacement reactions. The standard pattern: add Cl₂ to KBr solution and observe that Br₂ (brown color) is produced. Add Br₂ to KI solution and observe that I₂ (purple color) is produced. This directly tests your understanding of the reactivity series. For numerical problems, NEET often gives you concentrations and asks you to predict whether a reaction occurs or calculate equilibrium constants.

The displacement reaction hierarchy is unbreakable on NEET: F₂ displaces Cl₂, Br₂, and I₂; Cl₂ displaces Br₂ and I₂; Br₂ displaces I₂; but I₂ displaces nothing. Memorize this in terms of oxidizing power. When a halogen is a strong oxidizing agent, it can strip electrons from halide ions of less reactive halogens, converting them to elemental form.

Interhalogen Compounds

NEET occasionally tests interhalogen compounds like ClF, BrF₃, and I₂Cl⁶. These form when two different halogens react. The general formula is XY_n, where X is the larger halogen and Y is the smaller (more electronegative) one. Important for NEET: these compounds are even more reactive than pure halogens. If you see a question asking "which is the most reactive halogen compound?", interhalogen compounds are the answer. However, NCERT treats this topic lightly, so expect 1-mark questions or true/false statements rather than detailed numerical problems.

⚠️ Common NEET Mistake:

Students often confuse "most reactive halogen" with "strongest oxidizing agent" and think they're different things. They're not. F is both the most reactive and strongest oxidizer. Don't fall into the trap of a question that says "F is the strongest oxidizer but Cl₂ is more reactive in solution"—this is false. Always remember: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂ in both reactivity and oxidizing power.

Noble Gases: Inertness and Reactivity Exceptions

Noble gases (Group 18) are on the opposite end of the reactivity spectrum. NCERT Class 12 Chapter 10 teaches that noble gases have complete valence shells (8 electrons for all except He, which has 2), making them extraordinarily unreactive. For decades, chemists assumed noble gases simply didn't form compounds. NEET tests your knowledge of the breakthrough: in 1962, Neil Bartlett synthesized XeF₄, proving that the heaviest noble gases could form compounds under specific conditions.

Why this matters: NEET asks about noble gas compounds primarily to test your understanding that reactivity increases down any group. Helium is completely inert; xenon (Xe) can form compounds because its valence electrons are so far from the nucleus and so loosely bound that they can be involved in bonding. This shows up as questions like: "Which noble gas is most likely to form compounds?" Answer: Xe (or Rn, if given as an option). "Why does Xe form compounds when He and Ne don't?" Answer: larger size, lower ionization energy, more polarizable electron cloud.
